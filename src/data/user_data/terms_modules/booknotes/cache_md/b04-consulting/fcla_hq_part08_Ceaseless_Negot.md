#### Small-Step Project Approach

Background context: In contracting scenarios where there is some pressure or resistance from clients, it might be useful to take a small-step approach. This involves working with the client on a smaller piece of the total project so that the client can decide whether the process is as bad as feared.

The objective is to manage the contract in such a way that both parties feel more comfortable and confident moving forward. This often helps in reducing initial concerns and building trust, especially if the client has a positive experience with your services from the start.

To address client resistance or pressure, you should propose working on a smaller piece of the total project. This allows the client to experience your work and process first-hand before committing fully. By doing so, you can gauge whether the client's concerns are valid or not.

For example, if the client is hesitant about a large-scale audit, suggest starting with a pilot audit on a specific department or system. This way, you both get an opportunity to see how well the process works in reality.

#### Managing Client Concerns

Background context: When working under pressure, it's essential to acknowledge the situation and address the client's concerns directly. Clients often worry about losing control or feeling vulnerable during projects.

The objective is to find ways to proceed that minimize these fears while still ensuring you achieve your objectives. This involves negotiating a process that respects both parties' interests and comfort levels.

:p How can you manage client concerns by setting up the project in a way that preserves their sense of control?
??x
To manage client concerns, ask whether there is any way to proceed that would minimize their feelings of losing control or feeling vulnerable. For example:

- **Control Over Process**: Ask: "Is there some way we can set this up so that you don’t feel like you have given up too much control over the situation?"
- **Protection from Vulnerability**: Ask: "Is there any way we can ensure your vulnerability is protected during this project?"

By negotiating these terms, you can create a more collaborative and less threatening environment. This often involves finding compromises where both parties are satisfied.

For instance, if the client is worried about losing control over their data, you could agree to involve them in key decision points or provide regular updates that allow them to influence the process.

#### Shifting Dynamics of Consulting Projects

Background context: Consulting projects occur within dynamic and political organizational environments where people and pressures are constantly changing. This can lead to shifts not only in project scope but also in how clients perceive their roles.

The objective is to adapt to these changes by maintaining open lines of communication and being flexible with role definitions. The social contract between you and the client remains crucial, influencing the success of your work.

:p How do you handle changing dynamics within consulting projects?
??x
Handling changing dynamics involves recognizing that projects can shift due to various factors such as new management, budget cuts, or emerging issues. It's important to stay flexible and adapt to these changes by:

1. **Monitoring Cues**: Notice subtle changes in how the client interacts with you (e.g., missing meetings, rescheduling).
2. **Engaging in Negotiation**: Be prepared to renegotiate your role based on new circumstances.
3. **Maintaining Open Communication**: Keep the lines of communication open and transparent.

For example, if a new manager arrives who has different expectations or priorities, discuss these changes openly with them. Adapt your approach accordingly while ensuring that you still meet project objectives.

#### Defining the Problem to Death
In conducting consulting skills workshops and watching hundreds of consultants try to deal with a resistant client, one mistake stands out clearly: consultants spend too much time in the contracting meeting trying to define the problem. If we have a 1-hour meeting, we will spend 50 minutes understanding the problem and leave only 10 minutes for negotiating wants and dealing with concerns of control and exposure.

This happens because during the meeting, we can take on the confusion or obstinacy of the client. We aren’t sure what to do next, so we keep asking questions about the problem. It provides a nice relief for us and the client.

:p What is the key advice given regarding defining problems in contracting meetings?
??x
The key advice is to avoid spending too much time defining the problem during the initial contracting meeting. Instead, consultants should focus on negotiating wants and managing concerns of control and exposure more effectively.

If you find yourself spending excessive time understanding the problem:
1. **Stop Talking About the Problem:** When unsure what to do next or if the meeting isn't progressing, suggest a shift in focus.
2. **Ask Direct Questions:** For example, "Let's stop talking about the problem for a moment. Tell me what you want from me."
3. **Make Statements:** Start making statements that lead to action, such as "You seem confused about the nature of this problem," followed by providing guidance on how to proceed.

By limiting discussion of the problem to no more than 35% of the total meeting time and focusing on practical next steps, consultants can ensure they are making progress towards defining their role and objectives in the project.

#### Offering Value to the Client
Background context: Understanding what you are offering the client, both technically and personally, is crucial for establishing a clear value proposition during the contracting meeting. This includes considering facilitation roles that might help improve how they work together.

:p What are you offering the client in terms of technical expertise and personal support?

Offering technical expertise involves detailing what specific skills or knowledge you bring to the table. Personal support could include interpersonal skills, guidance on team dynamics, or other non-technical assistance that can enhance project outcomes.

Example questions:
- "What unique technical solutions can we provide?"
