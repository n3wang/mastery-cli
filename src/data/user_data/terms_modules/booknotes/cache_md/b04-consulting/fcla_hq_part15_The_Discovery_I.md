#### The Layers of Analysis in Problem-Solving

The text suggests that every problem has multiple layers, much like an onion. Each layer provides a different perspective on the root cause or solution. Initially, problems are often described at a high level (the presenting problem), which can be overwhelming and discouraging for both the client and consultant.

To effectively address these issues, it is essential to peel back each layer by asking specific questions that focus on different aspects of the situation:

1. **Top Layer**: Focuses on the technical or business problem.
2. **Second Layer**: Examines how others are contributing to the need for change.
3. **Third Layer**: Analyzes one's own role in sustaining the current state.

By working with these layers, you can move from a broad description of the issue to more actionable steps that address the underlying causes.

??x

1. The client has control over changing.
2. Are clearly important to the organization.
3. Have some commitment within the client’s organization to work on them.
4. Are a clear strength or an expansion of something currently working or under discussion.

#### Avoiding Collusion in Problem-Solving

Background context: This part of the text discusses avoiding two common mistakes that can undermine a consultant's effectiveness. These include developing explanations for problems that place blame outside the client’s control and downplaying difficult relationships, which might impact problem-solving.

:p What are the two main ways consultants can undermine their own influence by colluding with clients?

??x
Consultants can undermine their influence in two primary ways:
1. By developing explanations for problems that leave solutions outside the client's direct control. This often involves blaming external factors like higher management, economic conditions, or other groups within the organization.
2. By downplaying the impact of difficult relationships on problem-solving. Clients might face issues with subordinates or bosses but fail to address these in their reports.

To avoid collusion:
- Acknowledge roles others play in the problem while focusing the conversation on client responsibilities.
- Address difficult relationships directly without judgment, as this can significantly influence problem resolution and organizational dynamics.

For example, if a company is struggling due to poor communication between departments, highlight both internal factors (lack of clear directives) and external factors (team conflicts), but emphasize the importance of improving internal communication strategies that the client controls.
