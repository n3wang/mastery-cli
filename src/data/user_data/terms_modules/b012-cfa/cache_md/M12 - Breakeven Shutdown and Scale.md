Short Run and Long Run
- The short run for a firm is the time period over which some factors of production are fixed (land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship).
- The long run for a firm is the time period over which all factors of production are variable.


Shutdown and Breakeven
Example: Consider a retail store with a one-year lease and one employee as fixed costs. The variable costs are the store's cost of merchandise.
- The breakeven output quantity is when total revenue just covers both the fixed and variable costs.
- The price equals the average revenue, and the average total cost and the economic profit is zero.

Perfect Competition: Shutdown and Breakeven

Perfect Competition: Shutdown Points
- Short-run shutdown point: average revenue < variable cost (If the average revenue is > the average variable cost in the short run, continue to operate.)
- Long-run shutdown point: average revenue < average total cost
- Breakeven point: average revenue = average total cost (If the average revenue is $\geq$ the average total cost, stay in the market in the short and long run.)

Imperfect Competition
- Under conditions of imperfect competition, price is a variable under the firm's control and is a function of quantity (e.g., a monopoly).
- Price-searcher firms face downward-sloping demand curves, and marginal revenue is no longer equal to price.
- Breakeven analysis and shutdown points for such firms are based upon total costs and total revenues:
- $\mathrm{TR}=\mathrm{TC}$ : breakeven
- TC > TR > TVC: continue in short run, shut down in long run
- TR < TVC: shutdown in short run and long run

Total cost / total revenue approach:
- TR increases at a constant rate
- Total cost = total revenue $\mathrm{Q}_1 \& \mathrm{Q}_2$

Example: Short-Run Shutdown Decision
- Legion Gaming reported total revenue of \$700,000, total variable costs of \$800,000, and fixed costs of \$400,000 for the last fiscal year.
- Should the firm continue to operate in the short run?

Short-Run Shutdown Decision Solution
Breakeven and shutdown analysis
- $T R=T C$ : breakeven
- TC > TR > TVC: continue in short run, shut down in long run
- TR < TVC: shut down in short run and long run

Legion Gaming

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \operatorname{TR}(\$ 700,000)<\operatorname{TC}(\$ 800,000+\$ 400,000=\$ 1.2 \text { million }) \\
& \operatorname{TR}(\$ 700,000)<\operatorname{TVC}(\$ 800,000)
\end{aligned}
$$


The firm should shut down, and will lose $\$ 400,000$ in fixed costs



Example: Long-Run Shutdown Decision
- Legion Gaming reported total revenue of $\$ 850,000$, total variable costs of \$800,000, and fixed costs of \$400,000 for the last fiscal year.
- Should the firm continue to operate in the long run?

Long-Run Shutdown Decision Solution
Breakeven and shutdown analysis
- $T R=T C$ : breakeven
- TC > TR > TVC: continue in short run, shut down in long run
- TR < TVC: shut down in short run and long run

Legion Gaming
TR (\$850,000) < TC (\$800,000 + \$400,000 = \$1.2 million)
TC ( $\$ 1.2$ million) > TR ( $\$ 850,000$ ) > TVC ( $\$ 800,000$ )
The firm should shut down in the long run



Optimum Scale of Operations
- In the short run, the plant size is fixed-but in the long run, firms can choose their most profitable plant size.
- Economies of scale result from labor specialization, mass production, and investments in more efficient equipment and technology. The minimum efficient scale is where the average total cost is at a minimum and may be relatively flat or constant.
- Diseconomies of scale result from the increasing bureaucracy and inefficiency of larger firms.

## Characteristcs of Market Structures

Market Structure
- Market structures fall along the spectrum from perfect competition to pure monopoly.
- Factors that determine market structure
- Number of firms and their relative sizes
- Degree of product differentiation
- Bargaining power of firms to set prices
- Barriers to entry or exit the industry
- The degree of nonprice competition

| Number of sellers | Perfect Competition | Monopolistic Competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
|  | Many firms | Many firms | Few firms | Single firm |
| Barriers to entry | Very low | Low | High | Very high |
| Nature of substitute products | Very good substitutes | Good substitutes, but differentiated | Good substitutes or differentiated | No good substitutes |
| Nature of competition | Price only | Price, marketing, features | Price, marketing, features | Advertising |
| Price power | None | Some | Some to significant | Significant |

Perfect Competition
- Firms in perfect competition are price takers that take the equilibrium market price (example: market for wheat in a region).
- Characteristics
- Large number of firms, producing identical products
- Each firm is small relative to the total market
- Perfectly elastic demand curves
- No barriers to entry or exit
- Supply and demand determine market price

![[Pasted image 20251003221853.png]]


Monopoly Characteristics
- A monopoly market is characterized by a single seller of a product with no good substitutes (example: utility companies are a pure monopoly).
- Characteristics
	- The firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve (the market demand curve)
	- The firm has the power to set the price
	- High barriers to entry
	- Control over a resource needed to produce the product
	- Supported by government regulation or specific laws

![[Pasted image 20251003222029.png]]


#### Monopolistic Competition
- Monopolistic competition differs from perfect competition in that products are differentiated and not identical (example: toothpaste).
- Characteristics
	- Large number of firms in industry, low barriers to entry
	- Each firm has a small market share, collusion is not possible
	- Relatively elastic demand, downward sloping
	- Firms compete on price, quality, and marketing
	- Brand names, product innovation, marketing, and advertising

![[Pasted image 20251003222052.png]]


![[Pasted image 20251003222221.png]]



Oligopoly Characteristics
- An oligopoly market only has a few firms in the industry. Each firm must consider the actions and responses of other firms in setting prices and business strategies (a.k.a. interdependence).
- Characteristics
- Products may be similar (e.g., oil industry)
- Products may be differentiated (e.g., automobile industry)
- Products are often good substitutes for each other
- Significant barriers to entry (e.g., scale of operations)


Oligopoly Models
Oligopoly models of pricing and profits
1. Kinked demand curve model
2. Cournot duopoly model
3. Stackelberg dominant firm model
4. Nash equilibrium model


![[Pasted image 20251003222959.png]]
Cournot Model
- In Cournot's duopoly model, two firms with identical MC curves each choose their preferred selling price, based upon the price the other firm chose in the previous period.
- Duopoly: an oligopoly with two firms
- The long-run equilibrium is for both firms to sell the same quantity, dividing the market equally at the equilibrium price
- Firms assume that competitor's price will not change
- Market price lower than monopoly, higher than perfect competition


Nash Equilibrium
- The Nash equilibrium is reached when the choices of all firms are such that no other choice makes any firm better off (increased profits or decreased losses).

Example:
- Consider two firms, A and B . Each firm can either charge a high price or a low price. The choices are as follows:
- Both firms charge a high price, both charge a low price, one high and one low, and vice versa (see next slide)

Oligopoly Profits With Collusion
- Firms can earn a greater profit if they collude: fix industry output at the monopoly quantity and share the profits (example: the OPEC cartel).
- If competitors cannot detect cheating, a firm can increase profits if it violates the collusion agreement and increases output.

Collusion will be more successful with:
Fewer firms - Homogeneous products - Similar cost structures
- Certain and severe retaliation for cheating - Little competition from firms outside the agreement

Oligopoly: Dominant Firm Model
- A single firm has a significantly large market share because of its greater scale and lower cost structure (a.k.a. the dominant firm, $D F$ ).
- The market price is determined by the DF, and the other competitive firms (CF) take this market price as well.
- A price decrease by one of the competitive firms, which increases $Q_C$ in the short run, will lead to a decrease in price by the DF, and CFs will decrease output and/or exit the industry.
- The long-run result of such a price decrease by CF is to decrease the market share of CFs and increase the market share of the DF.

#### $\boldsymbol{N}$-Firm Concentration Ratio
Sum of the percentage market shares of the $N$ largest firms in an industry
- Market share = firm's sales / total market sales
- Lower ratios indicate competitive market; higher ratios indicate oligopoly

Disadvantages
- Ignores barriers to entry
- Largely unaffected by mergers


#### Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)
$\mathrm{HHI}=$ sum of squared market shares of $N$ largest firms in a market
- $\approx 0.1$ to $0.18 \rightarrow$ moderately competitive
- $0.18+\rightarrow$ uncompetitive market
- Advantages: more sensitive to mergers than $N$-firm ratio; widely used by regulators
- Disadvantages: ignores barriers to entry; ignores demand elasticity

#### Example: HHI (Before Merger)

The **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)** is calculated as:

$$HHI = \sum_{i=1}^N (s_i)^2$$

where $s_i$ = market share of firm $i$ expressed as a decimal.

Before merger (Acme 0.25, Blake 0.15, Curtis 0.15, Dent 0.10):

$$HHI = 0.25^2 + 0.15^2 + 0.15^2 + 0.10^2 = 0.1175$$

It shows a **moderately competitive market** (between 0.10 and 0.18).
