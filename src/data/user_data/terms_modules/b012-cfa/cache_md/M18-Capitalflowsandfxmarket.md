#### Market Participants – Hedgers and Speculators

Participants in the foreign exchange (FX) market differ in purpose: some seek to **reduce risk**, while others **take on risk** for potential profit.

:p Explain the difference between **hedgers** and **speculators** in the FX market.
??x

* **Hedgers:** Already have foreign exchange exposure (e.g., exporters or importers) and use **forward contracts** to lock in future exchange rates, reducing uncertainty.
* Example: A U.S. firm expecting EUR payments sells euros forward to avoid losses if EUR depreciates.
* **Speculators:** Have **no existing FX exposure**. They use forward contracts to **profit from anticipated exchange rate movements**.
* Example: A trader buys EUR forward, expecting it to rise in value.

#### Nominal vs. Real Exchange Rate

The **nominal exchange rate** reflects the market rate, while the **real exchange rate** accounts for inflation differences between countries.

:p Provide the formula for the **real exchange rate** and explain its meaning.
??x
Formula:
$$
\text{Real exchange rate}(p/b) = \text{Nominal exchange rate}(p/b) \times \left( \frac{\text{CPI}*{\text{base}}}{\text{CPI}*{\text{price}}} \right)
$$

* It measures **relative purchasing power** between two countries.
* If the real exchange rate rises, **domestic goods become more expensive** relative to foreign goods, reducing competitiveness.

#### Currency Appreciation and Depreciation

Changes in exchange rates indicate one currency’s value relative to another.

```python
def fx_change(start_rate, end_rate):
change = (end_rate / start_rate - 1) * 100
if change > 0:
	return f"Appreciation of {change:.2f}%"
else:
	return f"Depreciation of {abs(change):.2f}%"

print(fx_change(1.42, 1.39))  # Euro depreciation vs USD
# Output: Depreciation of 2.11%
```

\text{ change} = \frac{\text{End rate}}{\text{Beginning rate}} - 1 = \frac{1.39}{1.42} - 1 = -2.11%
$$
➡️ The **euro has depreciated by 2.11%** relative to the U.S. dollar.

Exchange Rate Regimes
The IMF lists exchange rate regimes in two categories: countries that (1) do and (2) do not have their own currency.

Countries that do not have their own currency
1. Formal dollarization: use the currency of another country (e.g., Zimbabwe and USD [2009-2019])
2. Monetary union: several countries use a common currency (e.g., countries in the European Union)

Exchange Rate Regimes (2)
Countries that have their own currency
1. A currency board arrangement: an explicit commitment to fix an exchange rate (e.g., HK currency, or HKD, is backed by the USD)
2. Conventional fixed peg: a country pegs its currency to within margins of $+/-1 \%$ to another currency/basket of currencies
- Direct intervention: monetary authority maintains the peg by buying and selling currencies in the FX markets
- Indirect intervention: via monetary policy, local regulation of FX

Exchange Rate Regimes (3)
Countries that have their own currency
3. Pegged exchange rates in a target zone: the permitted currency fluctuations (a.k.a. horizontal bands are wider; for example, +/- 2\%)
4. Crawling peg: a passive crawling peg adjusts the FX rate periodically to allow for inflation, and an active crawling peg announces a series of adjustments in advance
5. Crawling bands: the width of the bands identify permissible exchange rates over time; the wider the band, the greater the flexibility of monetary policy

Exchange Rate Regimes (4)
Countries that have their own currency
6. Managed floating exchange rates: use economic indicators such as inflation rates, balance of payments, unemployment data; intervention may be direct or indirect
7. Independent floating currency: the market determines the exchange rate, and foreign market intervention is only used to slow the rate of change and reduce short-term fluctuations; no specific target level for exchange rates

Changes in Exchange Rates
Changes in exchange rates affect both imports and exports.
If the USD/EUR exchange rate decreases:
- As the EUR has deceased, U.S. exports are more expensive and fall, while eurozone imports become cheaper and rise.

The effect on the goods and services market (imports and exports) will occur more slowly than effects on capital flows.


Balance of Payments
Capital flows offset any imbalance between the value of imports from, and exports to, another country.

Example: China and the U.S.
- Typically, the value of U.S. imports from China are higher than U.S. exports to China.
- The U.S. has a trade (goods and services) deficit with China, and China has a trade surplus with the U.S.-and capital flows offset this difference.

Trade Deficits and Balance of Payments
Trade deficits occur when imports exceed exports, so $(X-M)<0$.

Trade deficit impact on the balance of payments
The right-hand side must also be negative, so that total savings $[\mathrm{S}+(\mathrm{T}-\mathrm{G})]$ is less than domestic investment in physical capital (I):

$$
(\mathrm{X}-\mathrm{M}) \equiv(\mathrm{S}-\mathrm{I})+(\mathrm{T}-\mathrm{G})
$$


The additional capital needed to fund domestic investment must come from foreigners, creating a capital account surplus.

Objectives of Capital Restrictions
Governments may place restrictions on the flow of investment capital in and out of a country.
- Reduce the volatility of domestic asset prices, especially in a crisis, as capital outflows may drive down asset prices.
- Maintain fixed exchange rates (e.g., fixed rate targets).
- Keep domestic interest rates low by restricting the outflow of investment capital.
- Protect strategic industries for national security (e.g., telecommunications and defense industries).

#### Exchange Rate Regimes – Overview

Exchange rate regimes describe **how a country manages its currency relative to others**. The **IMF classifies them** into two broad categories based on whether a country **has its own currency** or not. Each regime affects monetary policy flexibility, inflation control, and economic stability differently.

??x
1. **Countries without their own currency** – they adopt another nation’s currency or share one collectively.
2. **Countries with their own currency** – they manage exchange rates through fixed, pegged, or floating systems.

#### Countries Without Their Own Currency

These nations **relinquish independent monetary policy** and rely on external or shared monetary authorities.

:p Explain the two exchange rate arrangements used by countries that **do not have their own currency**.
??x
1. **Formal dollarization:**

* The country adopts another nation’s currency (often the U.S. dollar) as **legal tender**.
* It cannot print money or conduct independent monetary policy.
* Example: **Zimbabwe (2009–2019)** used the **USD** after hyperinflation destroyed its local currency.

2. **Monetary union:**

* Several countries adopt a **common currency** governed by a **central authority**.
* Monetary policy is coordinated by a **shared central bank** (e.g., the European Central Bank).
* Example: **Eurozone countries** using the **euro (EUR)**.
 x??

#### Countries With Their Own Currency – Fixed Systems (1)

When nations retain their own currency, they can choose **how tightly they control its value** relative to others.

:p Describe a **currency board arrangement** and a **conventional fixed peg** system.
??x
1. **Currency board arrangement:**

* The government legally commits to exchange domestic currency at a **fixed rate** to a foreign anchor currency.
* The domestic money supply is backed by **foreign reserves**, limiting inflation and monetary expansion.
* Example: **Hong Kong Dollar (HKD)** pegged to the **U.S. Dollar (USD)**.

2. **Conventional fixed peg:**

* The domestic currency is pegged to another currency (or basket of currencies) within a **±1% margin**.
* **Direct intervention:** The central bank buys or sells currency to maintain the peg.
* **Indirect intervention:** Adjusting interest rates or regulations to influence FX demand.
 x??

---

#### Countries With Their Own Currency – Pegged and Crawling Systems (2)

Some countries prefer flexibility while keeping predictable stability.

:p Explain **pegged exchange rates in a target zone**, **crawling peg**, and **crawling bands** systems.
??x
3. **Pegged exchange rate in a target zone:**

* Similar to a fixed peg but allows wider fluctuations (e.g., ±2%).
* The central bank intervenes only when rates approach the boundaries.

4. **Crawling peg:**

* The exchange rate is **adjusted periodically** to reflect inflation differentials.
* **Passive crawling peg:** Adjustments happen automatically based on data (e.g., inflation).
* **Active crawling peg:** Adjustments are **announced in advance** to influence expectations.

5. **Crawling bands:**

* Combines crawling peg with **gradually widening bands**.
* The wider the band, the more **monetary policy flexibility** the country retains.
 x??

---

#### Countries With Their Own Currency – Floating Systems (3)

Floating regimes allow market forces to determine exchange rates, with varying degrees of intervention.

:p Differentiate between a **managed float** and an **independent float** exchange rate system.
??x
6. **Managed floating exchange rate:**

* The exchange rate moves according to **market supply and demand**, but the central bank **intervenes occasionally**.
* Decisions may be based on **inflation**, **balance of payments**, or **employment data**.

7. **Independent floating currency:**

* Exchange rate determined entirely by market forces.
* Central bank intervention only aims to **reduce short-term volatility**, not set a target rate.
* Example: **U.S. Dollar (USD)** and **British Pound (GBP)**.

#### Effects of Exchange Rate Changes

Exchange rate movements alter **trade flows** and **capital flows** between countries.

* Effects on **trade** (goods and services) appear more slowly than on **capital flows**, as financial markets adjust faster.

#### Balance of Payments – Concept

The balance of payments (BoP) records all economic transactions between a country and the rest of the world.

:p How do **capital flows** help maintain balance in international payments?
??x

* If a country imports more than it exports (a **trade deficit**), it must finance this difference.
* **Capital inflows** (foreign investment or lending) offset the deficit, keeping the BoP in equilibrium.
* Example: The **U.S. imports more from China** than it exports → U.S. runs a **trade deficit**, China runs a **surplus**, and **capital flows from China to the U.S.** (buying U.S. bonds) balance it.

#### Trade Deficits and Balance of Payments Equation

The BoP links trade balances to national savings and investment behavior.

:p Write the fundamental **balance of payments identity** and interpret its components.
??x
$$
(X - M) \equiv (S - I) + (T - G)
$$

Where:

* (X - M): **Net exports** (trade balance)
* (S - I): **Private savings minus investment**
* (T - G): **Government savings (budget balance)**

👉 When (X - M < 0) (trade deficit), the right side must also be negative:

* Domestic savings + public savings < domestic investment.
* The shortfall is funded by **foreign capital inflows**, creating a **capital account surplus**.
