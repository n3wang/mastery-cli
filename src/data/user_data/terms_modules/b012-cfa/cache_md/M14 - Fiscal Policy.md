Fiscal Policy
Fiscal policy refers to a government's use of spending and taxation to influence economic activity.
- A budget surplus occurs when government tax revenues exceed government expenditures.
- A budget deficit occurs when government expenditures exceed government tax revenues.
- A balanced budget occurs when government tax revenues equal government expenditures.


Government decisions on taxing and spending can be expansionary or contractionary.
- Expansionary fiscal policy is an increase in government spending and/or a decrease in taxes. This increases the budget deficit and increases aggregate demand.
- Contractionary fiscal policy is a decrease in government spending and/or an increase in taxes. This decreases the budget deficit and decreases aggregate demand

Monetary Policy
Monetary policy refers to the central bank's actions that affect the quantity of money and credit in an economy, to influence economic activity.
- Expansionary monetary policy occurs when the central bank increases the quantity of money and credit in an economy.
- Contractionary monetary policy occurs when the central bank reduces the quantity of money and credit in an economy.

Fiscal Policy
- Keynesian economists believe discretionary fiscal policy can stabilize the economy, increasing aggregate demand to combat recessions and decreasing aggregate demand to combat inflation.
- Monetarists believe that such effects are temporary, and appropriate monetary policy will dampen economic cycles.
- Automatic stabilizers (taxes and transfer payments) tend to increase deficits during recessions and decrease deficits during expansions.

Objectives of Fiscal Policy
Governments use fiscal policy to do the following:
- Influence the level of economic activity and aggregate demand
- Redistribute wealth and income among segments of the population
- Allocate resources among economic agents and sectors in the economy

Debt Ratio
- A country's debt ratio is the ratio of aggregate debt to GDP.
- When a country runs fiscal deficits, it incurs debt and interest to be repaid. Deficits are evaluated relative to annual GDP. High debt to GDP ratios may raise concerns about the solvency of a country.
- Real interest rates
- If the real interest rate on govt. debt $>\mathrm{GDP}$, the debt ratio increases.
- If the real interest rate on govt. debt < GDP, the debt ratio decreases.

Fiscal Deficit Concerns
Arguments for being concerned with the size of a fiscal deficit:
- Higher deficits lead to higher future taxes, which lead to disincentives to work and entrepreneurship, which leads to lower GDP
- If markets lose confidence in the govt., investors may not be willing to refinance the debt, leading to a govt. default, and printing money leads to inflation
- Increased govt. borrowing tends to increase interest rates, leading to firms reducing their borrowing and investment spending, reducing aggregate demand a.k.a. crowding out


Fiscal Deficit
Arguments against being concerned with the size of a fiscal deficit:
- If debt is primarily held by domestic citizens, the scale of problem is overstated
- If debt is used for capital investment, future economic gains will be sufficient to repay the debt
- Fiscal deficits may prompt needed tax reform
- If the economy is operating at less than full capacity, deficits can increase GDP and employment
- If private sector savings offset the govt. deficit (Ricardian equivalence)

Fiscal Policy Spending Tools
Fiscal policy tools include spending tools and revenue tools. Spending tools include:
- Transfer payments: a.k.a. entitlement programs are cash payments by government to redistribute wealth (e.g., Social Security benefits)
- Current spending: govt. purchases of goods and services on an ongoing and routine basis
- Capital spending: govt. spending on infrastructure and new technologies to increase future productivity (e.g. schools, roads, bridges and hospitals)

Justification for Govt. Spending
Government spending does the following:
- Provides services such as national defense
- Provides investments in infrastructure to enhance economic growth
- Provides a minimum standard of living
- Subsidizes investment in research and development for high-risk ventures (e.g., green technology)
- Supports the country's growth and unemployment targets

Fiscal Policy Revenue Tools
Direct taxes are levied on income or wealth (slow to implement):
- Income taxes, national insurance, wealth taxes, estate taxes, capital gains taxes, corporate taxes, and Social Security taxes

Indirect taxes are levied on goods or services (fast to implement):
- Sales taxes, value-added taxes (VAT), and excise taxes
- Indirect taxes can be used to reduce consumption of certain goods (e.g., alcohol and tobacco)


Desirable Attributes of Tax Policy
Desirable attributes
- Simplicity to enforce
- Not acting as a deterrent to working or interfering with market forces
- Horizontal equality: people in similar situations pay similar taxes
- Vertical equality: richer people pay more in taxes
- Taxes should generate sufficient revenues for govt. spending

Fiscal Tools: Advantages and Disadvantages
Advantages
- Fast implementation of social policies (e.g., discouraging tobacco use via indirect taxes)
- Increase revenue without significant additional costs

Disadvantages
- Direct taxes and transfer payments take time to implement
- Capital spending takes a long time to implement

Fiscal Multiplier
Initial government spending has a multiplied effect as it creates more spending. The magnitude of the multiplier depends on the tax rate and the marginal propensity to consume:

$$
\text { fiscal multiplier }=\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{MPC}(1-\mathrm{t})}
$$


For MPC = 0.8 and $t=0.25$, a \$100 billion spending increase, over time, can increase consumption by:

$$
1 /[1-0.8(1-0.25)] \times \$ 100=\$ 250 \text { billion }
$$


Tax Multiplier
- Changes in taxes also have a multiplied effect on aggregate demand.
- Example: If $\mathrm{MPC}=0.8$, a tax increase of $\$ 100$ billion will reduce consumption by $=0.8 \times 100=\$ 80$ billion. The fiscal multiplier effect will, over time, lead to a decrease in consumption spending of $2.50 \times \$ 80$ billion $=\$ 200$ billion.
- A $\$ 100$ billion increase in both spending and taxes can increase consumption spending by $\$ 250-\$ 200=\$ 50$ billion. The balanced budget multiplier is positive.

Discretionary Fiscal Policy
Fiscal policy is implemented through changes in taxes and spending (a.k.a. discretionary fiscal policy).
- Expansionary fiscal policy occurs when the economy is operating below full employment. During recessions, increase government spending or decrease taxes.
- Contractionary fiscal policy occurs during inflationary economic booms. Reduce government spending or increase taxes.

Fiscal Policy Limitations (1)
Discretionary fiscal policy is not an exact science:
- Economic forecasts might be wrong or misinterpreted
- Fiscal policy decisions: subject to three types of lags

Fiscal policy lags (destabilize fiscal policy changes)
- Recognition lag: takes time to recognize the economic problem
- Action lag: governments discuss, vote on, and enact policy changes
- Impact lag: time for corporations and individuals to act on the policy

Fiscal Policy Limitations (2)
Additional macroeconomic issues reduce the impact of fiscal policy:
- Misreading economic statistics
- Crowding-out effect as greater govt. borrowing tends to increase interest rates, which decreases private investments
- Supply shortages slow economic activity due to resource constraints
- There is a limit to expansionary fiscal policy (i.e., limits to deficits)
- If economy has high unemployment and high inflation, fiscal policy cannot address both simultaneously (stagflation)

Fiscal Policy Limitations (3)
- If economy is at full employment, fiscal stimulus will result in higher inflation
- If economy is below full employment due to supply shortages, fiscal stimulus will lead to inflation rather than GDP growth
