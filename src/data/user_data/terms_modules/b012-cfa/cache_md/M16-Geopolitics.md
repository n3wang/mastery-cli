Geopolitics
- The study of how geography affects international relations
- Interactions of governments (state actors), individuals, companies, and organizations (nonstate actors) with respect to economic, financial, and political activities
- Governments can be cooperative or noncooperative based on national interests; priorities influenced by geophysical resources
- Cooperation: trade flows, capital flows, exchange of information, culture, and information; soft power is influence without force

Globalization
Globalization: the long-term trend toward worldwide integration of economic activity and cultures (standardization of regulation)
- Countries that are connected to trade routes or that facilitate trade and capital flows between others tend to be cooperative, and may have power as a result.
- Land-locked countries rely on neighboring countries for access and trend toward cooperation.

Nationalism (anti-globalization): pursue national interests independently of, or in competition, with other countries


Cooperation and Globalization
- Autarky-noncooperation and nationalism: goal of self-reliance, producing domestically, low/no external trade or capital flows, state ownership of strategic industries (e.g., North Korea)
- Hegemony-noncooperation and globalization: open to global trade, influence; state control of key exports (e.g., Russia, U.S.)
- Multilateralism-cooperation and globalization: integrated globally, many trading partners, rules standardization (e.g., Germany)
- Bilateralism-cooperation and nationalism: significant cooperation with one other country, limited trade and capital flows with others

Nonstate Actors and Globalization
Nonstate actors may be more oriented toward globalization than their governments:
- Businesses looking outside their home country for profits, to reduce costs and to sell to new markets
- Investors seek to diversify outside their home country

Capital flows
- Portfolio investment flows: buying and selling foreign securities
- Foreign direct investment: physical production capacity in other countries

Geopolitical Risk
Geopolitical risk is the risk of events that interrupt peaceful international relations:
- Event risk-timing is known, outcome is unknown (e.g., outcome of a scheduled national election)
- Exogenous risk-timing and outcome unanticipated (e.g., outbreaks of war/rebellion, or a natural disaster)
- Thematic risk-known factors having effects over long periods (e.g., risk from terrorist attacks, climate change, cyber attacks)

Estimating Geopolitical Risk
Geopolitical risk affects investment values by increasing or decreasing the risk premium investors require to hold assets in a country or region.
- Probability: the likelihood of geopolitical risk
- Magnitude: the impact of geopolitical risk
- Velocity: speed of impact (e.g., high velocity is a short-term impact)
- Black swan risk: low-likelihood, high-impact, exogenous events

Cooperative and globalized countries have a lower risk of armed conflict, but a higher risk of supply chain disruptions (e.g., COVID-19).

Tools of Geopolitics
Tools used by state actors (governments) to advance their interests in the world:
- National security tools include armed conflict, espionage, bilateral and multilateral agreements, and alliances.
- Economic tools can be cooperative and noncooperative and includes free trade areas, common markets, economic, and monetary unions (also nationalization of companies and industries).
- Financial tools include foreign investment and the exchange of currencies (and sanctions on a geopolitical actor's financial interests).


Geopolitics and Investments
- Investors should consider if the impact of geopolitical risk is likely to be high or low and focus their analysis on high-impact risks.
- There is a discrete impact on a company or industry or broad impact on a country or region.
- The business cycle can affect the impact of geopolitical risks. Risks may have greater impacts on investment values during a recession.
- Scenario analysis is used to gauge the potential effects of political risks on investor portfolios (signals, a.k.a. signposts, of changes).

#### Python Analogy – Geopolitical Risk Assessment

```python
def geopolitical_risk(probability, magnitude, velocity):
risk_score = probability * magnitude * velocity
if risk_score > 50:
	return "High risk: consider reducing exposure."
elif risk_score > 20:
	return "Moderate risk: monitor developments closely."
else:
	return "Low risk: maintain positions."

# Example
print(geopolitical_risk(probability=0.4, magnitude=80, velocity=0.5))
# Output: "Moderate risk: monitor developments closely."
```

This logic models how investors evaluate **risk intensity** by combining likelihood, severity, and speed of geopolitical events.
