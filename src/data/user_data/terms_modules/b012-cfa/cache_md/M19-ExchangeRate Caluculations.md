No-Arbitrage: Spot and Forward Rates
- The no-arbitrage forward rate is driven by the differences in two countries' interest rates.

$$
\text { forward }_{d / f}=\operatorname{spot}_{d / f} \frac{\left(1+r_d\right)}{\left(1+r_f\right)}
$$

- The country with the higher interest rate, $r$, will see its currency depreciate (i.e., trade at a forward discount).


Calculate an Arbitrage-Free Forward Rate
Example
Consider two currencies, ABE and DUB . The $\mathrm{ABE} / \mathrm{DUB}$ spot rate is 4.5671 , the 1 -year riskless $A B E$ rate is $5 \%$, and the 1 -year DUB rate is $3 \%$. Calculate the 1 -year, no-arbitrage forward exchange rate.

Calculate an Arbitrage-Free Forward Rate (2)
Solution
- Note that the spot quote is given as ABE/DUB. This means the ABE rate is in the numerator, and the DUB rate is in the denominator.

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \operatorname{forward}_{A B E / D U B}=\operatorname{spot}_{A B E / D U B} \frac{\left(1+r_{A B E}\right)}{\left(1+r_{D U B}\right)} \\
& \operatorname{forward}_{A B E / D U B}=4.5671 \frac{(1.05)}{(1.03)}=4.6558_{A B E / D U B}
\end{aligned}
$$



Calculate an Arbitrage-Free Forward Rate (3)
Solution
- Note that ABE has the higher interest rate of $\mathbf{5 \%}$ relative to the 3\% rate of DUB.
- No-arbitrage suggests that the ABE currency will trade at a forward discount, and DUB at a forward premium.
- The approximate forward premium for DUB is $5 \%-3 \%=2 \%$.
- Forward premium $=(F / S)-1=(4.6558 / 4.5671)-1=1.94 \%$.

#### Foreign Exchange Rates – Cross Rates Overview

Cross rates are **implied exchange rates** between two currencies that are **not directly quoted** against each other in the market. Traders calculate them using a **common reference currency**—usually the USD or EUR. Cross rates are essential for **pricing**, **arbitrage**, and **international trade settlements** when no direct pair exists.

:p Define a **cross rate** and explain why it is important in FX markets.  
??x

- A **cross rate** is the **exchange rate between two non-USD currencies**, derived from their **exchange rates with a third common currency** (e.g., USD).
    
- They are important when:
    
    1. No active FX market exists for a currency pair.
        
    2. Traders need to convert indirectly through a common intermediary (often USD).
        
- Formula pattern:  
    $$\text{Currency A / Currency B} = \text{(Currency A / USD)} \times \text{(USD / Currency B)}$$

#### Cross Rates – Example 1 (MXN/AUD)

This example uses **USD** as the common currency to find the exchange rate between the **Mexican peso (MXN)** and **Australian dollar (AUD)**.

:p Given MXN/USD = 10.70 and USD/AUD = 0.60, calculate the **MXN/AUD cross rate**.  
??x

1. Set up so the **USD cancels**:  
    $$\frac{\text{MXN}}{\text{AUD}} = \frac{\text{MXN}}{\text{USD}} \times \frac{\text{USD}}{\text{AUD}}$$
    
2. Substitute values:  
    $$= 10.70 \times 0.60 = 6.42$$
    

 **MXN/AUD = 6.42** → One Australian dollar equals **6.42 Mexican pesos**.

#### Cross Rates – Example 2 (CHF/NZD)

Here, we use **USD** as the intermediary again, but one quote must be **inverted** before multiplying.

:p Given CHF/USD = 1.7799 and NZD/USD = 2.2529, calculate the **CHF/NZD cross rate**.  
??x
1. Find USD/NZD (reciprocal of NZD/USD):  
$$\text{USD/NZD} = 1 / 2.2529 = 0.44387$$
2. Set up to cancel USD:  
$$\frac{\text{USD}}{\text{NZD}} \times \frac{\text{CHF}}{\text{USD}} = \frac{\text{CHF}}{\text{NZD}}$$
3. Multiply:  
$$0.44387 \times 1.7799 = 0.7900$$
    

**CHF/NZD = 0.7900** → One New Zealand dollar equals **0.79 Swiss francs**.

#### Step-by-Step Logic for Cross Rate Calculation (Algorithmic View)

```python
def cross_rate(base_quote, price_quote):
    """
    base_quote: rate of base currency vs USD (e.g., MXN/USD)
    price_quote: rate of USD vs target currency (e.g., USD/AUD)
    returns: cross rate (e.g., MXN/AUD)
    """
    return base_quote * price_quote

# Example 1
mxn_usd = 10.70
usd_aud = 0.60
print(cross_rate(mxn_usd, usd_aud))  # Output: 6.42
```

This pseudocode demonstrates how traders align quote orientations so the **intermediate currency cancels out**, leaving a valid cross rate.

#### No-Arbitrage Condition – Spot and Forward Rates

The **no-arbitrage forward rate** prevents risk-free profit opportunities between **spot FX markets** and **interest rate differentials** across countries.

:p Provide the **formula** for the no-arbitrage forward rate and explain its economic meaning.  
??x  
Formula:  
$$  
\text{Forward}_{d/f} = \text{Spot}_{d/f} \times \frac{(1 + r_d)}{(1 + r_f)}  
$$

Where:
- ( r_d ): interest rate in **domestic** country
- ( r_f ): interest rate in **foreign** country
- ( d/f ): domestic currency per unit of foreign currency
    

**Interpretation:**
- If ( r_d > r_f ): domestic currency is expected to **depreciate** (forward discount).
- If ( r_d < r_f ): domestic currency is expected to **appreciate** (forward premium).

This ensures that investors earn **the same return** on risk-free deposits whether investing domestically or abroad—**no arbitrage**.

#### Arbitrage-Free Forward Rate – Concept

An **arbitrage-free forward rate** ensures that investors earn the same return whether they invest in domestic or foreign assets, after accounting for interest rate differences. This condition is called **covered interest rate parity (CIRP)**.  
If forward rates diverge from this equilibrium, **arbitrageurs** could exploit the discrepancy for risk-free profit — but competitive markets eliminate such opportunities quickly.

:p State the **no-arbitrage forward rate formula** and define its variables.  
??x  
$$
\text{Forward}_{d/f} = \text{Spot}_{d/f} \times \frac{(1 + r_d)}{(1 + r_f)}  
$$ 
Where:

- ( $r_d$ ): domestic risk-free interest rate
- ( $r_f$ ): foreign risk-free interest rate
- ($\text{Spot}_{d/f}$  ): current spot rate (domestic currency per unit of foreign currency)
- ( $\text{Forward}_{d/f}$ ): implied future exchange rate ensuring no arbitrage
    

✅ **If ( r_d > r_f )** → domestic currency **depreciates** (forward discount)  
✅ **If ( r_d < r_f )** → domestic currency **appreciates** (forward premium)

#### No-Arbitrage and Interest Rate Parity (IRP)

This principle ensures consistent pricing across global markets.

:p What happens if the **forward rate deviates** from the no-arbitrage condition?  
??x

- If the forward rate is **too high**, investors borrow in the foreign currency and invest domestically for profit.
    
- If **too low**, they borrow domestically and invest abroad.
    
- These trades continue until prices adjust and **interest rate parity (IRP)** is restored.
    
- In real markets, minor deviations may persist due to **transaction costs, capital controls, or regulatory frictions**.

#### Code Analogy – Forward Rate Calculation

```python
def forward_rate(spot, rd, rf, days=360):
    """Compute arbitrage-free forward FX rate."""
    F = spot * (1 + rd * days / 360) / (1 + rf * days / 360)
    premium = (F / spot - 1) * 100
    return round(F, 4), round(premium, 2)

# Example: 90-day ABE/DUB
spot = 4.5671
rd, rf = 0.05, 0.03
forward, premium = forward_rate(spot, rd, rf, 90)
print(forward, "ABE/DUB | Premium:", premium, "%")
# Output: 4.5898 ABE/DUB | Premium: 0.5 %
```

This illustrates how traders compute an **arbitrage-free forward FX rate**, ensuring **interest rate parity** and **no riskless profit opportunities**.
